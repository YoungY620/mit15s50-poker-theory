# L6 Poker Economics

This is a guest lecture by Aaron Brown of AQR Capital Management, on "Poker and Finance": why the game of poker was born, what it did economically, and how a generation of players carried the thinking of the card table into modern finance.

## A Land That Should Never Have Produced Fortunes

The story begins in the first half of the nineteenth century, in what was then called the Northwest of the United States — roughly the land bounded by the Missouri, Mississippi, and Ohio river systems. It was sparsely populated and desperately poor: essentially no roads or towns, full of swamps, mountains, and rivers that made overland travel extremely difficult, and more or less under continuous warfare from 1800 to about 1880 — large-scale campaigns in what would be called the Indian Wars, but also raids by dozens or by half a dozen men, and individual murders. It was among the poorest places on earth. Its inhabitants were the displaced of the continent: east coast Native Americans pushed west, local tribes resisting them, French-speaking Canadian rebels driven out by the English, insurgents pushed west after the Whiskey Rebellion, escaped slaves, and debtors. There was an expression at the time: if you didn't want to pay your debts, you wrote "GTT" — Gone To Texas — on your door and headed this way. In short, a land of losers, violence, and almost no economic resources.

Yet when someone compiled a list of the 100 wealthiest Americans of all time, estimating fortunes relative to the size of the economy, **19 of them were in this region in 1850**, having made their money between roughly 1850 and 1880. And not just robber barons like Rockefeller, Mellon, and Carnegie — great inventors like McCormick, Westinghouse, and Pullman, and innovators across industries: Swift, Pulitzer, Hearst, Armour, Marshall Field. All are household names today; the speaker challenges anyone to name a comparable cluster of business innovators anywhere else in the world in the entire twentieth century.

What did this land have? Not labor — it was sparsely populated, workers spent most of their time eking out subsistence, and labor was extremely expensive. The speaker's answer: **they had poker**.

## A Designed Game

The first written records about poker from outside this region date to the late 1820s and early 1830s. What they don't say is more interesting than what they say. None of them explain the rules of the game; none even call it a "game." The one thing they are clear about is that **it involved transferring large amounts of money**. Nobody said it was a variant of some game played elsewhere; nobody said the French, the Indians, or the Persians brought it. It was simply there — unique to the region — and it was common knowledge: the locals played a serious game called poker, for very large stakes, and it was played nowhere else.

Far more popular than poker at the time were the pure games of chance played in variants all over the world by people of every class — dice, faro, wheel-based games. Aristocrats played whist and chess: games of skill that did not necessarily involve gambling. Poker was neither. So what was different about it, compared to every game that came before?

**Ace beats king.** Trivial today, revolutionary then — in most countries of the world, saying the ace outranks the king could get you executed. In early poker the ace was always high; there was no low/high ace. The designers, evidently, were not monarchists.

**Hands rank by rarity.** The rarer the hand, the higher it ranks; early poker had no straights or flushes, so the principle operated even more directly. But one later change is revealing. Today, comparing two hands of the same type (two pair against two pair, high card against high card), we compare starting from the highest card down. Before the 1830s it was the reverse — **you compared the lowest cards first**. In the table below, of the 1,302,540 five-card hands with no pair, no straight, and no flush, 502,860 have an ace as the highest card — the most common type ranks highest, which inverts the "rarer is stronger" principle. Ranking by the lowest card is the version faithful to it.

This difference drastically devalues the ace, much like lowball poker: your best cards don't help you; your worst card determines your hand's strength. Holding A, K, Q, J with a 2 is a "2 low" and loses to nearly everyone. The numbers quantify the gap: when both players hold nothing hands, the player with an ace wins about $83\%$ under modern ranking; ranked by low card, the ace is worth only about $56\%$, because the differences among ace, king, queen, and jack are tiny.

The speaker's main point is not the strategic subtlety, but this: **the game was designed** — someone articulated a rational principle and did what was, at the time, fairly sophisticated mathematics to set the hand rankings. It did not evolve through long tradition, and no court official wrote its rules. Someone designed it, for a purpose.

## The Betting Design of Straight Poker

Early poker — called straight poker at the time — was almost trivially simple in its dealing sequence: one card dealt, a betting round; a second card, a betting round; a third card, a betting round; and the last two cards — already called the turn and the river, the same names used in hold 'em today — were dealt together, with **no betting after the final card**.

The betting mechanism was equally carefully designed: no ante, and no blind in the modern sense. The player to the dealer's left was called the "age." The age posted a stake before seeing his first card, of any amount he chose, **including zero**. Only the first round differed from modern poker: **you were not allowed to call the age's bet** — you had to fold or raise, with a minimum raise of double the stake (if the age posts \$1, you must put in at least \$2). All later rounds worked like modern poker. This made the age strictly stronger than today's small blind: a blind can be called, but the age's bet could only be doubled or surrendered.

Players at the time insisted that **poker is not gambling**, and the distinction was this: no one is ever forced to make a bet. Having seen your cards, you voluntarily stake money on what you believe — in today's terms — has positive expected value. Posting the age's stake was empirically regarded as advantageous — mathematically it can be shown this cannot be true, but psychologically and empirically it was: the others were forced to double or fold, and everyone else bet only when they thought they would win.

## Settled Every Round: Mark-to-Market

Poker betting had another feature no earlier game possessed: **at the end of every betting round, every player remaining in the hand has bet exactly the same amount**. The hand is marked to market after each round — ultimately you are betting on who has the best hand at showdown, but the game cannot continue until every surviving player's wagers are equalized.

## The Rules Loosen and Evolve

The strict rules did not last. By the 1840s and 1850s people were rebelling against the discipline: adding antes, straights and flushes, betting after the final card, and developing variants such as draw poker and stud poker with some cards exposed. John Blackbridge, in *The Complete Poker Player* (1875), criticized the ante:

> The ante converts poker into a lottery, with this condition superadded that the lottery system does not have — every player must buy a ticket.

About a hundred years after the events, R. F. Foster explained the evolution in *Practical Poker* (1904):

> The scientific players have themselves to thank for driving their less wary opponents to the ante and the limit. Most people want to play, whether they hold court cards or not; sitting out hand after hand waiting for court cards is no fun, even when doing so carries an advantage of two in eleven in money terms... The conservative players of the old school would not go in without court cards, and kept throwing in hand after hand without investing a chip, until the liberal players could find no one to bet against them.

And so a conservative, old, strictly scientific game evolved into modern poker: much more fun, explicitly permissive of gambling, and serving a rather different purpose.

## Poker Checks and Credit Creation

Neither the cards nor the betting was poker's most revolutionary part. Throughout history — and gambling goes back to prehistory, existing in virtually every culture — gambling was done for goods or cash; when credit was used, it was provided by a trusted central counterparty, such as the organizer of the game. Poker was never played for cash or goods. It was played for what were then called "poker checks." As late as the 1980s people still clearly distinguished two often-confused things: **checks had intrinsic value** — Las Vegas casinos once used casino checks in place of cash for every transaction; **chips were merely markers** — bought at the table and properly cashed back in when leaving, not even to be carried from the roulette table to the craps table. Later, when the IRS came down and casinos severed ties with organized crime, legal chips with intrinsic value disappeared: today, if you show up with a \$5,000 chip to redeem, the casino will demand proof that you bought or won it there, and will otherwise refuse to pay.

Back to the early 1800s. Poker checks were markers the players made themselves: typically small clay disks pressed with a thumbprint or some other identifiable personal mark. The key point is that **the markers were tied to individuals** — you were playing poker with money you created yourself. If you lost, others held claims on you; if you won, you held their markers. After a session, players performed "ring clearing": you traded the other players' checks you held for your own checks back; a winner ended up holding a pile of other people's checks, meaning many people owed him money. **There was no central counterparty; collection was the winner's own responsibility** — if you didn't like a man's checks, too bad; your only recourse was finding someone else to trade with. This created a form of money, and this credit creation is the truly essential element of early poker.

Compare a contemporary financial institution: the soft money bank, otherwise called the wildcat bank. Someone announced "I have a bank," started making loans, and either printed bank notes — some extremely crude, with markers, twigs, and old tally sticks pressed into service — or simply kept a set of accounts; anyone wanting to spend the money first had to deposit it into the bank. If it worked, it generated a great deal of economic activity: loans were repaid, deposits honored. If it didn't, everything became worthless. Add one feature and you get what most people think of as a bank: **capital in the form of real money — gold**. The speaker insists on this dichotomy: in his view, the classic bank *is* the soft money bank, and capital is merely an add-on that lends credibility — first, it shows the banker has skin in the game and takes a loss if the bank collapses, a signal of confidence; second, in theory it compensates part of the losses if the bank fails (in practice it never does — bankers always get their capital out ahead of everyone else). Much banking legislation and popular intuition take the opposite view, treating a "100%-capitalized" bank as the norm and fractional reserve as the add-on. Across human cultures you find forms of this self-credit-creation almost everywhere — susus, tontines, and the like — and poker was one of them: you created your own money, played a game, and if you won you picked up a mass of other people's credit with which to start economic activity; if you lost, your creditors had to find work for you to get their money back. A great deal of business in the Old West was created this way. As the old-time gambling boss Big Julie Weintraub put it: "The guy who invented poker was bright, but the guy who invented the chip was a genius."

## The Futures Exchange: A Second Invention from the Same Soil

Moving forward to the 1840s: the Chicago Board of Trade appeared.

![The Chicago Board of Trade in session (c. 1900). Source: Library of Congress, public domain](assets/ch06-1.png)

Futures exchanges appeared in **exactly the same geographic region** as poker, about twenty years later. And likewise: nobody claimed to have invented it; exchanges sprang up everywhere inside the region and nowhere outside it; and it was unlike any financial institution that had existed before — there was something in this culture that made an unprecedented thing seem natural. The analogies to poker are obvious: **mark-to-market** — you are betting on the price of wheat three months out, but you settle up daily so both sides always have the same amount at stake; **clearing** — the early exchanges used ring clearing exactly as in poker, later evolving into the more sophisticated clearinghouse.

What are futures exchanges for? The textbook answer is "farmers use them to lock in prices." Take that story seriously for a moment. You are a farmer in 1840, a two-week journey from Chicago over bad roads. Since the beginning of private agriculture, farmers have sold to crop buyers — the buyer's agent is at the place where you buy supplies and rides out to your farm every month to check the crop; you can lock in a price with him, and he will buy your entire crop, whatever quantity you produce, at a price set now or later, delivered by you or picked up at your farm for a slightly lower price. Now compare the "innovative" futures market: you must travel two weeks to Chicago; promise delivery of a fixed quantity and fixed grade of wheat that you don't produce and can't be sure of assembling on time; you don't know your yield but must specify the quantity in advance; you must post initial margin while all your cash is tied up in the crop (farmers only have cash after harvest); and you must stay in Chicago every day for mark-to-market settlement — so who is growing your wheat? It makes no sense at all. Anyone who writes in a textbook that "farmers hedge with futures" has not thought about it for half a second: no farmers were involved in founding the exchanges; farmers were suspicious of them and repeatedly tried to have them shut down; and when farmers do use futures markets, they are almost always buyers, not sellers.

The canonical trade was done by processors (and everyone who founded those exchanges was a poker player — look it up). I am a miller; I grind wheat into flour. I cannot hedge "wheat risk" with futures, because I have no natural exposure to the price of wheat: if a great war breaks out in Europe and other regions' crops fail, wheat demand and price soar — and my business booms, because wheat pours in from everywhere, grinding capacity runs short, and I can raise my prices; in that sense I am long wheat. But if the local crop fails and wheat spikes on scarcity, nobody needs my mill — there is excess grinding capacity and I can't charge a penny. What I actually care about is my machinery, fuel, labor, regulation, and the quality of my input; if I want to lock prices with suppliers or buyers, I can sign contracts anytime — I don't need an exchange.

The real use of the futures market is this: I first contract with a grain silo operator (a middleman who buys wheat from all over and sells to processors) for staggered deliveries over the next three months — specified times, quantities, and varieties, delivered to my mill; then I go to the futures exchange and **sell an equal amount of wheat forward**. Now I have eliminated price risk: whether wheat rises or falls is irrelevant to me — I bought wheat today and sold it three months out; **I have borrowed wheat**. I could instead have borrowed money to buy wheat, but then I'd bear two layers of risk: the risk of money itself (remember, this is a region short of gold and silver, with a weak banking system and untrustworthy bank notes — taking money risk was itself a big risk) and the risk of wheat's price. The simplest course is to borrow the wheat directly. And as with any business loan, I never intend to repay it: just as a machinery loan is refinanced at maturity, I will **roll these futures contracts forever** and never take delivery — I have permanently borrowed part of my business input instead of buying it. First, then, the futures exchange created a tremendous amount of credit. Second, it quoted prices for services nobody had ever conceived of: who knows how to turn flour back into wheat, or how to move August wheat back to May? For 175 years the Chicago Board of Trade has quoted both of those services. Want to build a bridge that diverts wheat bound for Saint Louis into Chicago? You can buy Chicago wheat and sell Saint Louis wheat, selling that transportation service on the futures market. Calendar spreads, grade spreads, cleaning spreads — all are ways of buying and selling the services inside the agricultural processing chain. This was a far more efficient way to organize things — internally generated credit, far better information flows — and it touched off the explosion of business and innovation that spread across the world. Oddly, futures long remained confined to agricultural commodities, despite the existence of far more valuable things; only in the 1970s, when the same idea was moved into finance, did things truly take off.

One common myth should be dispelled: futures did not evolve from "to arrive" contracts. To arrive contracts predate written records — they are common in the earliest legible clay tablets of ancient Babylon: "when wheat arrives at the market, I will sell you 10,000 bushels at \$0.50 a bushel." It is a **price guarantee, not a delivery guarantee**: it does not say when the wheat will arrive, or even promise that it will. The largest to arrive market in the United States was in Buffalo — have you ever heard of fortunes won and lost on the Buffalo to arrive exchange, the fistfights, the corners? No. To arrive contracts were run by quiet commission clerks; futures exchanges were populated by tough, brawling innovators who made and lost fortunes and went on to dramatic enterprises. There is no lineage between the two.

A telling side observation: of Buffalo, Chicago, Cincinnati, Omaha, and Texas, which are names of poker games? Omaha is, Texas is, Chicago is, and Cincinnati is (though rarely played today) — but there is no poker game called Buffalo. In fact, **every poker game named after a place is named for a place where, if you lost all your money and drowned your sorrows by jumping in the river, you would float down to New Orleans**. Even today, poker remains strongly a regional attitude: an extraordinarily explosive, innovative economic attitude that has never really seeped out beyond its birthplace. Internet poker may change that, but it leaves an open question: is the soul of poker alive? Will this economic innovation and freedom, this self-credit-creation, spread worldwide through online poker — or, once poker moves onto the computer and becomes virtual, has its soul been extracted?

## From the Card Table to Wall Street

The speaker was born in the 1950s and raised in Seattle. Outsiders would call his childhood idyllic (his father was a professor; the family was neither rich nor hard up), but he hated the era: he sincerely believed the world would end in nuclear war before he got to college; the math and science he loved was funded almost entirely by defense projects; more than half the world lay under totalitarian rule, and no country had ever emerged from communism into freedom and prosperity; and the economy was a mess — Seattle slid into the "'70s malaise" years before the rest of the country, and a family friend who was the world's leading expert in materials for supersonic wing design (an MIT man) was laid off because nobody was building supersonic planes anymore and ended up driving a cab. The social atmosphere was equally strange: as long as a man cut his grass and brought home a paycheck he was a good guy, and nothing else (drinking, domestic violence, drugs) was ever discussed; but if a man lost his job, the family would quietly move away and never be mentioned again. The message he absorbed: if you could earn money, everything was fine; if you couldn't, the consequences were unspeakable.

He was a shy, introverted kid, but he was fascinated by the numbers in the back of the newspaper, worked out their patterns, bet on horses and sports, and found he could win. At the time, taverns all over the American West had poker games in the back room or the basement; he would walk in, win, collect, and walk out — and this was enormously liberating: **you don't have to get a job, you don't have to go to college; anywhere in the world you can find a game and win; and once you're known as a good player, even if you go broke, people will lend you money and stake you**. Nor did the games look like the movies: there were policemen, sailors, clerks — ordinary people of every kind — and if you were good, these people ended up owing you money.

![The interpersonal network a poker game constitutes: the game as an exchange node for credit and services](assets/ch06-2.png)

He later worked out his function in this network. A good player serves the game in two ways: first, he **protects the game** — if an outside shark shows up to take everyone's money, the locals have a strong player of their own (and "being good" in those days meant far more than card skill: you had to spot cheating, judge who was at risk of arrest, and who might turn violent); second, he **connects the game to a larger network**. The core of the game was credit creation: doctors, lawyers, policemen, and mechanics exchanged services in a vast underground economy — a lawyer with no practice, because he owed money around the game, would still get letters to write and bits of legal work. Some people had dropped out of normal life entirely, drew no paycheck, and subsisted wholly on the underground economy; the game was a vital organizational tool — a sailor could bring in Cuban cigars, a bathroom attendant at a fancy restaurant could fence them. Poker mattered because you spent long stretches of time with these people and came to know them deeply; you couldn't fake it — an undercover cop could blend in for two days, but no one plays poker every day for years as an act.

Carrying this network with him, he came to Boston for Harvard, and was astonished to find the network **translated seamlessly across the country**. On the subject of "going to college to make contacts," he contrasts his three roommates (a corporate lawyer, a TV producer, a law professor) with the Harvard poker games, which included future president George W. Bush, Bill Gates, Steve Ballmer, the writer Scott Turow, and other celebrities, politicians, and rich men. **A poker connection is not a friendship; it is a business relationship**, and his entire career was shaped by poker networks. In his recollection, the two best players in the Harvard games of that era were Turow (who had pro-level instincts, just not enough hours at the table) and the mathematician Lloyd Trefethen (later of Oxford, who taught him a good deal of theory); Bush played mostly for fun and connections, and ran a great game.

In a high-stakes Boston game, a traveling pro who managed a card room near Stanford told him: your mathematical sense for the game resembles the crowd in Gardena, California — "I can win in the top game of any city in the country, but I can't turn a profit in Gardena; you should go test yourself there." Gardena was then the best poker in the world (the movie *California Split* (1974) contains actual scenes shot in the Gardena card rooms of that era), and it was the network economy on steroids. This was the late 1970s: marginal tax rates were extremely high, regulation was stifling, the tax code was complicated and corrupt; community property law had wiped out many men in divorces; many carried tax liens. The players were far better educated than the Seattle or even Boston crowd — smart, broke, and **financially toxic**: money in a bank would be whisked away, cash in the pocket could be legally seized, but poker chips could not; and poker debts owed to fellow players were unenforceable by anyone. So the underground economy was hyperactive: on his first day, a motel manager (the former owner, kept on by the bank after going bankrupt) offered rooms to poker players for a fraction of the rent slipped to him under the table; after three or four months you'd be "evicted," the books would record that the tenant never paid, and you'd move to the next motel in the cycle. Car repairs, lawsuits, surgery — someone in the card room would do it, all under the table, with money borrowable on the spot. The founders of modern poker theory — David Sklansky, Mason Malmuth, Mike Caro, the first people to write poker theory books — all came out of this scene. Las Vegas at the time hated poker (the reason is simple: someone in the building was losing money and the casino wasn't getting it), so casinos either had no poker room or stuck it under the stairs next to the slot machines, opening and closing it at random; the World Series of Poker of that era was a pure casino publicity stunt — only in the late 1990s and early 2000s, when the internet, television, and the poker boom brought in new blood, did casinos truly embrace poker.

Another comparison conveys Gardena's level: three WSOP champions — Doyle Brunson, Sailor Roberts (the only truly top-tier player of that crowd), and one other — used to drive around Texas playing the local games. Three world champions should have cleaned up, but Sailor told the speaker **they only broke even at poker**; the real money was in collecting bets on high school football — they were the "bag men," and poker was just the door-opener. Conversely, an ordinary Gardena club player who could break even in the big game was theoretically miles ahead of those Texas road gamblers: the latter were very good at making a living from poker, but asked to explain why they played a hand a certain way, they talked pure luck, with no strategic logic; whereas a Gardena player dropped into a Texas tavern would have been knocked out with a pool cue in five minutes — making a living and understanding the game are two entirely different things.

## Beating Roulette: A Lesson About "Randomness"

The speaker made his living at poker, but his interests were broader. His deepest impression from four years in Boston was how shoddy the quantitative analysis in academia and policy was — terrible data, stupid analysis, and **none of these people would bet a nickel on their own conclusions**; the same man would apply far more commonsense analysis to buying a car than to recommending that the government take over the steel industry. He and his kindred spirits believed in quantitative methods and were confident they could prove themselves by winning bets — among gamblers, any opinion you voice can be met with "put some money on it," which cuts the empty chatter dramatically. The natural proving ground for methods was wherever people gamble.

They had all read Ed Thorp's *Beat the Dealer*. Thorp, a mathematics professor, invented blackjack card counting, beat virtually every casino game, and invented or perfected almost all of the quantitative hedge fund strategies in use today; he was also one of the most successful hedge fund managers for many years. The speaker focuses on roulette — it permanently changed how he thinks about many things, and it is an extremely important lesson for anyone with an academic statistics background.

How do you beat roulette? There were two schools of thought at the time: one said to record the outcomes and look for biases (say, 13 coming up too often); the other said the wheels were built too well to be biased. Thorp's insight was that **you can win either way**. If the wheel is biased, just bet 13; but if the wheel is so perfect that every number comes up equally often, it must be machined to perfection — and then you can use Newtonian physics to compute where the ball will land. He built the device with MIT's Claude Shannon, the father of information theory. The principle: the wheel spins one way while the ball rolls the other way around the rim of the bowl. The ball's travel along the rim is fully Newtonian and predictable — with the electronic aids that were legal at the time (now illegal), it is easy to compute which number will be directly under the ball at the moment it leaves the rim; the bouncing between that moment and rest is nearly chaotic and cannot be predicted point by point. But the key insight is: **the predictable segment can be calculated precisely, and the chaotic segment is nowhere near uniform on the wheel** — if 17 is under the ball when it comes down, you can compute the distribution of its final resting places, and it is far enough from uniform to make profitable bets (in practice the idea requires several layers of refinement; only the outline is given here).

This connects directly to what Nassim Taleb calls the "ludic fallacy": classical statistical theory is built on devices like dice that are engineered to produce randomness, while the real world is nowhere near that random — **even a casino cannot manufacture randomness on purpose**. The reason: build a device precisely enough and it is predictable; build it loose and sloppy and it has non-uniform patterns; a device that is both precise and unpredictable exceeds human capability. The "randomness" of the stock market, politics, and war is modeled far too sloppily. Whenever someone says "this is random," the right response is: cut it open, find the small pockets of predictability you can calculate exactly, and find the non-uniform patterns in between. To outsiders this looks strange: you obsess over data and data quality in corners everyone else considers irrelevant, while waving your hands and making "criminally crude" assumptions about the things everyone considers vital. But that is exactly how you win — **the things you think matter don't, and the things you think don't matter do**.

## Three Roads into Finance

The people who cracked the casino games (blackjack, roulette, baccarat, craps) and the people who did sports betting walked two different roads. Casino games are frequentist work: you are taking the casino's money, and once spotted you can expect anything from a warning to a burial in the desert — so you must fool the casino and stay under the radar; it suited antisocial loners and required no social skills (the theatrical MIT card-counting teams of the movies were not the norm; most just walked in quietly and played).

Sports betting is Bayesian work: **predicting how people will bet is far easier than predicting how a game will come out**. In the 1970s a single observation was enough to make money — the Los Angeles Lakers were the most glamorous team and Los Angeles a big betting city, so when the Lakers played at home the incoming money pushed the point spread too far, and you simply bet against the Lakers at home; no genius, math PhD, or computer required. Patterns like this rest on understanding people: the malcontents went to the casino games, the people persons went to sports betting. Success was also treated differently: casinos threw you out, but bookmakers **hired you** — paid you a salary (in the form of letting you bet bigger), made you a partner, and eventually you ran your own book. Both camps had acquired, outside any classroom, techniques generations ahead of academic statistics and econometrics — because they had to be right: they were betting every day, and the smartest people in the world were spending every waking moment trying to beat the system.

Poker sat in between: there was a card-play component (counting, shuffle reading) and a people component — you didn't have to predict thousands of people as in sports betting, but you had to read the players at your table, get invited to games, collect from losers, and avoid arrest, cheating, and violence. In the 1970s and early 1980s, many of this generation carried their honed skills into finance, each to his natural place:

- **The casino-game people** went into secretive little hedge funds: their own money plus a few wealthy investors, returning outside capital as soon as they could, doing extremely deep work in narrow niches.
- **The sports-betting people** went into the big banks: they understood people and business, and were natural bank executives. In those days you left math off your résumé when applying to Wall Street — Wall Street didn't want people who knew math, just as the NFL doesn't want math PhDs; some feared smart people were dangerous, others assumed anyone trained in math had to be an ivory-tower nerd. But once inside, the knowledge accumulated over five or six winning years in sports betting — about markets, business, and running people — was more than many learned in a lifetime.
- **The poker people** (the speaker's kind) could get jobs, but didn't want the top posts: they wanted to run small departments under the radar — quant trading, pairs trading, structured products. They were good at networking but not at wearing a suit and pleasing bosses, nor at raising money, so they attached themselves to big firms: the firm supplied trading capital and existing relationships, and they kept (part of) what they made.

These people revolutionized Wall Street. To convey how deep the change in finance from the early 1980s to now has been, the speaker offers an analogy: a digital camera and a chemical camera look alike — both run on batteries, have a flash, a lens, a shutter, and take the same pictures — but the technology inside is entirely different, and making one requires completely different people and processes. The technological basis of finance was rebuilt wholesale in just this way, and the intellectual "genetics" of that generation of quants owes more to sports betting, casino games, and poker than to the economic theory of the 1970s and 1980s.

The divide persists. Wall Street is fundamentally sales: the people who raise money and gather assets always take the lion's share, while quants earn "small money" at the edges. When the early quants arrived, Wall Street's attitude was "we don't care — you could be astrologers or chartists; as long as you generate trades and pay us commissions, we'll pretend to respect you"; when it turned out these people were actually making money, the attitude became wary and dismissive at once. To this day, when a quant fund like AQR undergoes credit review, the counterparties assume "nobody can understand your black box" and routinely assign their most junior credit staff — even though the strategy can be laid out completely in eight sentences. The biggest barrier is the **culture of confidence**: non-quants measure your credibility by how confident you appear, and they understand the man who says "I'm sure — I have 87 reasons" (privately discounting him to 51%); when a quant honestly says "I'm about 60% confident in this trade," the listener doesn't know what to make of it. Quants understand that 51% with enough bets is a cash register; non-quants struggle with the concept — which is why they're playing roulette without the glasses.

- James McManus, *Cowboys Full* — the only decent general history of poker (the author also wrote *Positively 5th Street*, about reaching the final table of the WSOP Main Event).
- Jeffrey Williams, *The Economic Function of Futures Markets* — for about a century everything written about futures markets was nonsense; this thin book from around 1990 is logical, self-consistent, empirically supported, and the first real explanation of futures markets — yet almost nobody cites it.
- Perry Mehrling, *Fischer Black and the Revolutionary Idea of Finance* — how rationality and mathematics entered finance through eccentrics like Fischer Black (the speaker wrote the foreword to the paperback; Mehrling also wrote *The New Lombard Street* on modern finance).
- Daniel H. Usner, Jr., *Indians, Settlers, & Slaves in a Frontier Exchange Economy* — the "prehistory" of the lower Mississippi valley before poker (up to about 1791): that pre-modern economy already contained more of the design of the modern economy than London or New York of the same era.
- Sebastian Mallaby, *More Money Than God* — portraits of the early hedge funds; light on strategy detail, but rich in stories.
- Aaron Brown, *The Poker Face of Wall Street* — the speaker's own book on the poker–finance connection.
- Aaron Brown, *Red-Blooded Risk* — the speaker's own account of how the ideas of sports betting, poker, and casino games entered mainstream finance.
- David Hayano, *Poker Faces* — the author was a Gardena player and a sociology PhD (a self-described "autoethnographer"), and captured the 1970s Gardena poker economy in full; it was the last gasp of the old-style poker economy.
- Philip Tetlock, *Expert Political Judgment* — on how bad "experts" are at prediction: if you are good at testing judgment with real money, you must get away from experts and go somewhere that tells you, in cold hard cash, whether you are right or wrong.

## Key Takeaways

- The mid-nineteenth-century American "Northwest" (the Missouri–Mississippi–Ohio river basin) was a poor, violent frontier, yet between 1850 and 1880 it produced 19 of the "100 wealthiest Americans of all time"; the speaker argues the key factor was poker.
- Poker was not a folk game that evolved; it was **designed**: the ace always outranked the king (revolutionary at the time), and hands ranked by rarity (early poker even ranked by the lowest card to enforce this strictly — an ace-high nothing hand drops from about $83\%$ to about $56\%$ equity).
- Straight poker's betting design deliberately excluded forced bets: the age priced his stake freely, and the first round allowed raise-or-fold only; players of the day therefore insisted "poker is not gambling." At the end of every round all remaining players had bet equal amounts — the game was marked to market round by round.
- Poker checks were player-made, person-specific credit instruments, settled by ring clearing: money creation with no central counterparty. Together with wildcat banks, susus, and tontines, poker belongs to humanity's tradition of self-credit-creation, and it was an engine of business organization in the Old West.
- The futures exchange arose in the same region twenty years later, with mechanisms isomorphic to poker (mark-to-market, ring clearing); its true function was not farmer hedging but letting processors "borrow wheat" (buy spot, sell forward, roll forever), pricing services such as transportation, time, and grading in the processing chain, and generating enormous credit and innovation.
- Poker's underground network was simultaneously a credit network and a career network: good players protected games and connected networks; the speaker traveled it from Seattle taverns through Harvard games to the Gardena card rooms, inside a web of contacts spanning the country.
- Thorp and Shannon's roulette work shows that a device is either precise and predictable or sloppy and non-uniform — "perfect randomness" cannot be manufactured; look for exactly calculable pockets and the non-uniform patterns between them, rather than lazily assuming randomness.
- Casino games (frequentist, antisocial), sports betting (Bayesian, social), and poker (both) were three paths that respectively stocked hedge funds, big banks, and quant trading desks; the technological basis of modern finance was rebuilt by these gambler-turned-quants.
